\begin{abstract}
Does a quantum reservoir computer (QRC) forecast a chaotic system better than a classical model
when the comparison is fair? We fix four conditions: every classical baseline is tuned with a
search budget comparable to the QRC's, the classical models see the same input window and have the
same number of features, the QRC's headline number is read from a finite number of measurement
shots, and every result is a multi-seed statistic. Under these conditions we run eight cells on
one-step forecasting of a six-variable Lorenz-96 system with a twelve-qubit reservoir: two memory
schemes (reset and recurrent), two Hamiltonians (a disordered XXZ model and an all-to-all Ising
model) and two readouts (one measurement basis or two). In all eight cells the QRC loses to a
random-feature map of the same width and window on every one of five fresh data seeds. The best QRC
cell ties a degree-2 polynomial of the input window, and an echo-state network of the same width
wins every recurrent cell by a wide margin. A second, non-commuting measurement basis is the QRC's
largest gain but does not close the gap. In one single-basis XXZ pair per memory scheme, finite
shots cost the recurrent QRC far more than the reset one and turn a tie with the random features
into a loss. Neither a local refinement stage after the Bayesian search (tested in the best QRC
cell) nor widened parameter ranges (tested in the two-basis Ising cells) changes a verdict against
the random features or the echo-state network. In free-running forecasts no model's mean valid
prediction time reaches one Lyapunov time, and the QRC stays behind the random features and the
echo-state network. All tables are generated from the stored result records.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Quantum reservoir computing trains only a linear readout on the measured outputs of a fixed quantum
system driven by the input signal~\cite{fujii2017}. Because nothing quantum is trained, it is a
candidate for near-term hardware, and several studies report that a small quantum reservoir
forecasts chaotic or nonlinear time series better than a classical one~\cite{ahmed2024,
kobayashi2024,nguyen2026}. Whether such a result says something about the quantum system or about
the comparison depends on how the classical side was set up. We call a comparison fair when four
conditions hold:
\begin{enumerate}
\item every classical baseline is tuned with a budget comparable to the QRC's, by the same search
  method, on the same tuning seeds;
\item the classical models see the same input window as the QRC, and those built to match it have
  the same number of features;
\item the QRC's headline number is computed from a finite number of measurement shots, as hardware
  would report it, not from exact expectation values;
\item every reported number is a statistic over fresh data seeds and reservoir seeds that were never
  used in tuning, with paired tests corrected for multiple comparisons.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{What is new.} In earlier work~\cite{pandey2026cartography} we compared QRCs with
capacity- and tuning-matched classical models in noiseless statevector simulation at up to eleven
qubits, on coupled H\'enon maps and a regime-switching autoregression, and found that neither a
correlator readout nor measurement feedback gave the QRC an edge. A companion study tested whether a
high-dimensional quantum reservoir gains from its dimension~\cite{pandey2026qra}, and a third
compared fixed-reservoir and variational quantum models on Lorenz-63~\cite{pandey2026qpinn}. This
paper changes five things. Every QRC number is read from finite shots, and the cost of shots is
measured against exact values. A second, non-commuting measurement basis is added at the same total
shot budget, and the width-matched classical models grow with it. The tuning budget is matched by
search density (trials per tuned knob, run to convergence) rather than by trial count, and its
sufficiency is checked by a local refinement stage and by widened ranges. Our own records show a
verdict reversing when one ESN knob is added to the search. And every frozen model is also run as a
free-running forecaster.

\paragraph{Finding.} On one-step forecasting of Lorenz-96 with $K=6$ variables and a
$q=\nQubits$ qubit reservoir, the QRC loses to a random tanh feature map of the same width and window
on every data seed of all eight cells. Its best cell ties a degree-2 polynomial of the input window,
and an echo-state network (ESN) of the same width wins every recurrent cell. The free-running test
keeps this ordering.

\section{How advantage claims are usually made}
\label{sec:related}

Six patterns recur in QRC benchmarks. None makes a result wrong; each makes it answer a narrower
question than an advantage claim needs. We map papers to patterns only where their own text states
the relevant fact.

\paragraph{1. Resources counted in different units.} A quantum reservoir is often sized in qubits
and a classical one in neurons. Ahmed, Tennie and Magri~\cite{ahmed2024} find that on Lorenz-63
their best quantum architecture at 9 to 11 qubits reaches valid prediction times similar to those of
classical reservoirs of 500 to 2000 neurons, while the classical reservoir outperforms every quantum
architecture at the same reservoir size; three quantum architectures outperform it on a
ten-variable Lorenz-96 system as the size grows (their Figs.~8 and~12). What a linear readout sees is
the number of measured features, and we match that number directly.

\paragraph{2. Untuned or lightly tuned baselines.} The strength of a baseline depends on which knobs
are searched. Kobayashi, Fujii and Yamamoto~\cite{kobayashi2024} report the best ESN over a grid of
five spectral radii and four learning rates (their Appendix~D). Nguyen and Soh~\cite{nguyen2026},
forecasting the Mackey-Glass series with a driven Jaynes-Cummings reservoir, tune an ESN's spectral
radius, leak, input scale and bias over 810 settings on the training loss, while their quantum model
receives grid search, Bayesian search and a further QFI-aware optimisation stage.

\paragraph{3. QRC compared with QRC.} Careful work compares quantum reservoirs across dynamical
regimes~\cite{martinezpena2021} or measurement protocols~\cite{mujal2023}. That is how a reservoir
should be designed, but a gain over another QRC is not a gain over a classical model.

\paragraph{4. Capacity metrics in place of forecast error.} Hu et al.~\cite{hu2023} give a framework
for the expressive capacity of a quantum system under the sampling noise of finite measurement.
Capacity guides design; it does not say whether a tuned classical model forecasts the task better.

\paragraph{5. Exact expectations in the headline, shots elsewhere.} Hardware returns counts. The
results of~\cite{ahmed2024} come from noise-free emulation, with finite sampling left to future
work; \cite{kobayashi2024} state their main results under negligible statistical fluctuations and
treat a finite number of measurements in their Appendix~C; \cite{nguyen2026} obtain their margins
from expectation-value simulations and analyse shots in a supplementary note.

\paragraph{6. Few seeds.} A single reservoir draw on a single series gives no estimate of the
spread between draws. We do not attribute this pattern to specific papers; we report every
comparison over $5\times5$ evaluation runs.

Where strong classical baselines are included they often win: in the gate-based multivariate study of
Hamhoum et al.~\cite{hamhoum2025} the QRC matches classical reservoir computing on Lorenz-63, but a
nonlinear vector autoregression and a clustered ESN remain stronger in some settings.

\section{Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

All runs use the \texttt{qrc\_bench} pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:repro}); every constant below is read
from its source or from the stored records.

\paragraph{Task.} Lorenz-96 with $K=6$ variables on a ring,
$\dot x_k = (x_{k+1}-x_{k-2})x_{k-1} - x_k + F$ with $F=8$, is integrated by fourth-order
Runge-Kutta with step $0.01$; one sample is kept every $\sampleEvery$ steps, after 1000 discarded
samples, and each variable is standardised. A series has 2000 samples in the window cells and 3000
in the recurrent cells. The target is the next sample of all six variables. The score is the
one-step $\nrmse$ on the last 15 percent of the series, averaged over the six variables, each
normalised by its test-slice standard deviation, so that $\nrmse=1$ is the mean predictor. The
readout is fit once on everything before the test slice, after a washout of 50 samples (window) or
400 (recurrent).

\paragraph{Quantum reservoir.} The reservoir has $n_{\mathrm{in}}=6$ input qubits, one per variable,
and $n_{\mathrm{mem}}=6$ memory qubits. Each input $x$ is encoded as
$R_Y(\mathrm{clip}(s\,x,-\pi,\pi))|0\rangle$ with $s\in[0.1,\pi]$ tuned, and the system evolves for
a tuned time $\tau\in[0.1,10]$ under one of two all-to-all Hamiltonians,
\begin{align}
H_{\mathrm{XXZ}} &= \sum_{i<j} \left(X_iX_j + Y_iY_j + \Delta\, Z_iZ_j\right) + \sum_i h_i Z_i,
  \quad h_i\sim\mathcal{U}(-W,W),\\
H_{\mathrm{Ising}} &= \sum_{i<j} J_{ij}\, X_iX_j + v\sum_i Z_i, \quad J_{ij}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1),
\end{align}
with $\Delta\in[-2,2]$, $W\in[0.1,10]$ and $v\in[0.1,10]$ tuned and the disorder drawn from the
reservoir seed. In the \emph{window} cells the memory is reset: at every step the memory qubits
start in $|0\rangle^{\otimes 6}$ and the last two inputs are driven in order, the input qubits being
traced out between drives, so the QRC is a fixed feature map of a two-step window. In the
\emph{recurrent} cells the memory state is carried from step to step, and a feedback term shifts
every input angle by $k_{\mathrm{fb}}\tanh(\bar m_{t-1})$, where $\bar m_{t-1}$ is the mean measured
$\langle Z\rangle$ of the previous step and $k_{\mathrm{fb}}\in[0,8]$ is tuned. The Z/ZZ readout
gives $\langle Z_i\rangle$ and $\langle Z_iZ_j\rangle$ on all twelve qubits, $\widthZZ$ features;
the +X/XX readout measures the same block a second time in the $X$ basis, $\widthZX$ features. The
feedback signal and the memory state stay on the $Z$ basis.

\paragraph{Shots.} Every QRC feature in the eight cells is estimated from $1024\,q=\shots$ shots per
step, split equally over the bases, so the second basis costs no extra shots. We use the restarting
protocol~\cite{mujal2023}: each step's measurement is a fresh preparation of the same circuit, so the
memory state carried forward is exact, while the features and the feedback signal are read from the
sampled counts. The shot noise realisation is fixed by the reservoir seed.

\paragraph{Classical models.} \emph{Random features (RF)}: $\tanh(A z_t + b)$, where $z_t$ stacks
the same two-step window the QRC sees ($n=12$ values), $A_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2/n)$,
$b_i\sim\mathcal{U}(-\beta,\beta)$, $\sigma\in[10^{-3},10]$ and $\beta\in[0,10]$ tuned, with the
QRC's width. \emph{Poly2} and \emph{linear}: the complete degree-2 polynomial of the window (width
$\widthPoly$) and the window itself (width $\widthLin$), with only the ridge penalty tuned; both have
no reservoir seed. \emph{ESN}, recurrent cells only: a leaky echo-state network of the QRC's width,
$r_t=(1-a)\,r_{t-1}+a\tanh(W r_{t-1}+W_{\mathrm{in}}x_t+b)$, with spectral radius in $[0.05,1.5]$,
leak $a\in[0.01,1]$, input scale in $[0.01,5]$, density in $[0.02,1]$ and bias scale in $[0,10]$
tuned. The window models remain as references in the recurrent cells.

\paragraph{Tuning.} Every model is tuned by Optuna's TPE sampler~\cite{akiba2019} with the same
sampler seed. The ridge penalty $\alpha\in[10^{-6},10^{2}]$ (log scale) is tuned for every model, on
standardised features, and counts as a knob. The budget is matched by search density: a model with
$d$ knobs gets at least $25d$ trials and continues until 50 trials pass without a new best, with a
cap of 300; every model in the eight cells stopped on the patience rule. The objective is the
validation $\nrmse$ over three rolling-origin folds (each 10 percent of the series, all before the
test slice) and over three tuning data seeds $\times$ three tuning reservoir seeds. Each model's best
setting is frozen and evaluated once on five fresh data seeds $\times$ five fresh reservoir seeds.

\paragraph{Statistics.} For each QRC-versus-classical pair we form the paired difference on each of
the 25 runs and on each of the 5 data-seed clusters. We count the runs and data seeds the QRC wins
(differences within $5\times10^{-4}$ are ties) and apply a paired $t$-test to the cluster means,
Holm-corrected within the cell (three comparisons in a window cell, four in a recurrent cell). No
correction is applied across the $\nTests$ tests of the grid; the random-feature verdict survives
even a Bonferroni factor of $\nTests$ (largest corrected $p$: $\bonfRf$), the two weakest Poly2
results (Holm $p=\polyWeakPLo$ and $\polyWeakPHi$) may not. For Poly2 and linear the five reservoir seeds of a data seed share one prediction,
so only the cluster-level test is independent; we use it throughout.

\label{sec:shots}

\subsection{Free-running forecasts}
\label{sec:vpt}

We ran every frozen model autonomously from $\nStarts$ starts in the test slice: the model predicts
the next sample from the true history and then feeds each prediction back as its next input
(recurrent models are first warmed on the true data; the QRC draws fresh shots every step). The valid
prediction time (VPT) is the time before the error, the RMS over variables of the error scaled by
each variable's test-slice s.d., first exceeds $\vptThreshold$, within $\horizonLt$ Lyapunov times.
The largest Lyapunov exponent of the task's own integrator (Benettin's method on the same RK4 step) is
$\lambda=\lyapExp$, so a Lyapunov time is $\lyapTimeSteps$ samples; the same routine gives $1.70$ for
$K=40$, close to the value of about $1.68$ reported for that system~\cite{vlachas2020}. No model's
mean VPT reaches one Lyapunov time (Table~\ref{tab:vpt}; the ESN is longest at $\vptBest$), so these
one-step readouts are poor autonomous forecasters at this sampling. The ordering against the
stronger models holds: in every cell the random features outlast the QRC (significantly in
\vptSigRfCells{} of 8; Appendix~\ref{app:stats}), and in every recurrent cell the ESN outlasts both.
With one basis the QRC falls to the level of the simpler models, tying the linear model in the
window cells and Poly2 in the recurrent cells; with two it outlasts Poly2 on every run, because Poly2
diverges when fed its own output (mean $\nrmse$ $\polyNrmseOneLtWindow$ after one Lyapunov time in the
window cells). The coarse sampling also makes these horizons far shorter than those of
Ahmed et al.~\cite{ahmed2024}, who sample every $0.01$ time units and report VPTs of up to about $8.5$
Lyapunov times on Lorenz-63; our VPTs compare the models of this grid with each other, not with that
work.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What would it take for the QRC to win here?} Halving the shots per basis to add a basis
helped in every cell, and most in the window cells, which suggests that there the limit is what is
measured rather than how precisely. The gain is confounded with the doubled width, since we did not
run a one-basis QRC of width $\widthZX$, and the width-matched classical models gain from width too;
a third basis must add more than width does. Wider ranges improved the QRC only within the
verdicts it already had (Section~\ref{sec:refine}).
On hardware the restarting protocol is costly for the recurrent QRC: the circuit of step $t$
replays all $t$ drives, so one series of $\recSteps$ steps needs about $\replayDrives$ drives per shot
round, each repeated $\shots$ times; the reset QRC needs two drives per step. The recurrent QRC is
thus the more expensive scheme and, with two bases, not the better one. Weak-measurement and
rewinding protocols~\cite{mujal2023} reduce the replay cost at the price of extra noise. Finally, the
models are tuned for one step; a fair long-horizon test would sample finely and tune every model on
the free-running metric.

\paragraph{Why earlier claims differ.} The six patterns of Section~\ref{sec:related} each predict a
direction in our grid. \emph{Resource units}: at equal feature width the random features win.
\emph{Baseline tuning}: in our first exact-expectation run of the recurrent XXZ cell the ESN had no
bias term and reached $\noBiasEsn$ against the QRC's $\noBiasQrc$, the QRC winning \noBiasQrcWins{}
of 25 runs; adding the bias scale to the ESN's search, with the QRC's result unchanged, gives
$\biasEsn$ and an ESN win on every run. \emph{QRC against QRC}: the second basis is a real gain over
the one-basis QRC, which would read as a success against that baseline alone. \emph{Capacity
metrics}: we report forecast error only. \emph{Exact expectations}: Section~\ref{sec:shots} shows a
tie under exact values becoming a loss on every seed under shots. \emph{Few seeds}: the QRC's s.d.\
over runs is $\qrcSdMin$ to $\qrcSdMax$, so single-run gaps smaller than that are not interpretable;
the margins we report are larger and hold on every data seed.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

The study has one task (Lorenz-96, $K=6$, $F=8$), one system size ($q=\nQubits$), one input window
(two steps), two reservoir families with all-to-all couplings, one shot budget, and one tuning
horizon (one step). The free-running test uses coarse sampling, so its VPTs resolve only a few
steps, and a single error threshold. The shot-noise contrast rests on one single-basis XXZ pair per
memory scheme, the feedback-noise mechanism is not tested by an ablation, and the refinement stage
and the widened ranges were each tested in one or two cells. The Holm correction is applied within
cells, not across the grid. Shots are sampled from the exact state, with no gate, readout or
decoherence noise, so hardware would be harder on the QRC, not easier. For scale, the no-change
forecast scores $\nrmse$ $\persMin$ to $\persMax$ on the test slices: at this sampling the last value
is a worse forecast than the mean. The results say nothing about larger reservoirs, other encodings,
quantum input data or other tasks.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}

All runs use the \texttt{qrc\_bench} pipeline
(\url{https://github.com/pandey-tushar/Quantum_Chaos_solver}). Each record stores its protocol, the
trial history of every model, the per-run test scores, package versions, the GPU and the pipeline's
git commit (Table~\ref{tab:repro}, Appendix~\ref{app:stats}). The runs used one NVIDIA GeForce
\gpu{} GPU with 4 GB of memory; the comparison records take $\totalHours$ hours of wall time in
total, almost all of it in the QRC's tuning, and the eight free runs $\freerunHours$ h. Two records
ran from changes later committed: the refinement record carries commit \texttt{\refineCommit}, its
SPSA stage committed as \texttt{df03eeb}; the free-run records carry \texttt{\freerunCommit}, the
free-run code committed as \texttt{752b50b}. The tables and inline numbers of this paper are
generated by \texttt{make\_tables.py} and its figures by \texttt{make\_figures.py}, both reading the
records directly.
